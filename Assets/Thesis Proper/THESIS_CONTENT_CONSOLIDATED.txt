=== CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION ===

--- Section 1.1: Background and Context ---

The Philippine Department of Education's adoption of the MATATAG (Momentum for Advancement of Teachers through Learning, Accountability, and Good Governance) curriculum represents a significant pedagogical shift toward competency-based learning frameworks aligned with international educational standards. Within the science domain, specific emphasis has been placed on developing students' understanding of abstract scientific concepts—particularly microscopic phenomena—that substantially challenge learners' intuitive conceptual models. Phase change phenomena (melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition) are exemplary abstractions: everyday observational experience provides limited access to particle-level mechanisms underlying macroscopic state transitions. Students develop persistent misconceptions—for example, the belief that liquid water disappears rather than transforms to vapor, or confusion regarding simultaneous heating and non-temperature-increase phenomena during phase transitions—that resist conventional instructional approaches.

+++ Subsection 1.1.1: Research Problem and Justification +++

Contemporary educational research increasingly emphasizes game-based learning as a pedagogical approach capable of engaging learners through intrinsic motivation mechanisms while simultaneously scaffolding complex conceptual understanding. However, implementation of educationally effective games remains constrained by multiple challenges: technical development resource limitations, institutional adoption barriers, and demonstrated difficulty in maintaining pedagogical rigor when prioritizing entertainment value. The confluence of these constraints is particularly acute in resource-limited educational contexts such as Philippine public schools, where budget constraints, infrastructure limitations, and limited access to technology create structural barriers to game-based learning adoption.

This study investigates the feasibility of developing a pedagogically sound, technically functional, educationally aligned game-based learning environment addressing a specific learning competency from the MATATAG Grade 6 science curriculum: students' understanding of phase-change phenomena. The research question guiding this investigation is: Can an educationally aligned, narrative-contextualized game-based learning environment effectively engage Grade 6 students while reinforcing their understanding of phase-change concepts aligned with MATATAG learning competencies?

+++ Subsection 1.1.2: Design and Development Motivation +++

The conceptualization of Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter emerged from the intersection of identified pedagogical need (reinforcing phase-change understanding through multisensory, interactive engagement) and technical feasibility considerations (game development approaches enabling meaningful learning experiences within resource constraints). The game's design operationalizes established educational theory—particularly outcomes-based edugame design frameworks and multimedia learning principles—to create learning experiences where gameplay progression intrinsically depends upon conceptual mastery rather than external incentive systems.

--- Section 1.2: Objectives of the Study ---

+++ Subsection 1.2.1: General Objective +++

To design, develop, and evaluate a 2D educational platformer game (Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter) that reinforces Grade 6 students' understanding of phase-change phenomena (melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition) aligned with the MATATAG science curriculum learning competencies.

+++ Subsection 1.2.2: Specific Objectives +++

1. To design game mechanics integrating lower-order platformer navigation systems with higher-order conceptual gameplay systems (particle lattice manipulation), ensuring pedagogical alignment with phase-change learning competencies.

2. To implement interactive particle visualization systems enabling students to directly manipulate and observe microscopic-level particle behavior during phase transitions, facilitating visualization of abstract scientific phenomena.

3. To evaluate game usability, learner engagement, and perceived pedagogical effectiveness through formative assessment (MEEGA+ Kids evaluation instrument) during iterative development cycles.

4. To conduct summative evaluation comparing pretest-posttest achievement across phase-change learning outcomes, assessing whether single-session gameplay exposure produces measurable improvements in standardized science assessments.

5. To document qualitative evidence of engagement, peer-mediated learning, and affective responses through thematic analysis of researcher field notes, enabling assessment of learning mechanisms beyond conventional achievement testing.

6. To identify design considerations, implementation challenges, and recommendations for future game-based learning development in Philippine educational contexts, particularly addressing resource limitations and infrastructural constraints.

--- Section 1.3: Scope and Limitations of the Study ---

+++ Subsection 1.3.1: Scope +++

This study focuses exclusively on the design, development, and evaluation of an educational game addressing a single learning competency: phase-change understanding. The study does not attempt generalization to broader game-based learning effectiveness; rather, it provides detailed documentation of one implementation within a specific educational context (Philippine Grade 6 science). The game targets students aged 11-12 years within the MATATAG curriculum framework, specifically addressing learning outcomes related to describing changes of state and associated particle behavior.

The research encompasses full development lifecycle documentation from initial design conceptualization through iterative refinement based on formative feedback, deployment across multiple platforms, and summative evaluation with student participants. Evaluation methodology integrates quantitative pre-post achievement testing with qualitative engagement analysis and usability assessment.

+++ Subsection 1.3.2: Limitations +++

1. Sample Size Constraints: Recruitment challenges resulted in limited participant numbers (3 formative participants; 18 summative participants) compared to initial study design (target N=30). This constraint substantially limits statistical power and generalizability of quantitative findings.

2. Single-Session Intervention Design: The study incorporated a single-session gameplay exposure (approximately 30 minutes) due to institutional availability constraints. Learning outcome consolidation typically requires distributed practice; single-session designs likely underestimate long-term learning potential.

3. Single-Site Implementation: Evaluation occurred exclusively at Inchican Elementary School due to recruitment constraints. Findings may not generalize across diverse Philippine educational contexts with varying infrastructure, student demographics, and institutional capacity.

4. Assessment Limitations: Summative evaluation relied upon existing school-administered science assessments (pretest-posttest design) with acknowledged ceiling/floor effects. Assessment design limitations may suppress observed achievement gains.

5. Temporal Assessment Issues: Posttest administration occurred during summer vacation following extended interval from initial phase-change instruction. Score degradation due to natural forgetting may obscure intervention effects.

6. Platform Limitations: Development was constrained to mobile browser-based deployment due to device availability constraints. Full-platform optimization (native Android/iOS apps, desktop implementations) was not feasible within scope constraints.

--- Section 1.4: Significance of the Study ---

This research contributes to understanding game-based learning implementation within resource-constrained educational contexts in multiple dimensions:

Pedagogical Significance: The study demonstrates technical and pedagogical feasibility of developing educationally rigorous games with finite resources addressing abstract scientific content. It identifies specific design mechanisms (narrative contextualization, progressive mechanic introduction, peer learning scaffolding) through which games support science learning.

Practical Significance: Documentation of development challenges, design decisions, deployment strategies, and iterative refinement processes provides pragmatic guidance for educators and instructional designers undertaking similar educational technology initiatives in Philippine contexts.

Methodological Significance: The integration of quantitative achievement assessment with rich qualitative engagement analysis illustrates assessment pluralism approaches that more comprehensively capture game-based intervention impacts than conventional testing alone.

Theoretical Significance: The study contributes to game-based learning theory by documenting the tension between quantitative null findings (standardized test scores) and robust qualitative evidence (engagement, peer learning, dispositional shifts), suggesting that game-based interventions operate through mechanisms substantially misaligned with conventional achievement testing.

=== CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW ===

--- Section 2.1: Historical and Contemporary Educational Context in the Philippines ---

The Philippine Department of Education has undergone successive curriculum reform cycles responding to evolving educational priorities and international benchmarking. The K-12 curriculum (implemented 2016-2017) established outcomes-based education frameworks emphasizing competency development alongside knowledge acquisition. The subsequent MATATAG curriculum revision (implemented 2024) represented renewed emphasis on learning outcome clarity, reduced redundancy across grade levels, and enhanced alignment with observable workplace and civic competencies. The MATATAG framework particularly emphasizes science competencies enabling students to understand natural phenomena through investigation-based approaches grounded in scientific method.

The transition between K-12 and MATATAG frameworks documented multiple implementation challenges: curriculum unpacking inconsistencies across teachers, variable pedagogical interpretation of competency statements, assessment design misalignment with intended learning outcomes, and variable resource allocation across schools. Research on K-12 implementation documented particular difficulty with abstract concept instruction in natural sciences, where traditional teacher-centered lecture approaches proved insufficient for developing robust conceptual understanding aligned with prescribed competencies.

--- Section 2.2: Game-Based Learning Theory and Research ---

+++ Subsection 2.2.1: Foundational Learning Theories +++

Game-based learning implementations leverage multiple foundational learning theories:

Constructivism emphasizes active learner construction of knowledge through experiential engagement rather than passive knowledge reception. Games inherently embody constructivist principles through iterative discovery, consequence-responsive gameplay, and learner-directed exploration. Bruner's work on discovery learning and intuitive knowledge development—the belief that learners construct conceptual understanding through repeated engagement with problem domains—provides theoretical foundation for game-based approaches wherein repeated gameplay cycles enable progressive conceptual refinement.

Experiential Learning Theory, articulated by Kolb, describes learning as cyclical iteration through concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. Game-based learning environments afford natural implementation of experiential cycles: gameplay provides concrete experience; reflection mechanisms (dialogue systems, interface design choices) scaffold reflective observation; game narratives and mechanics embody abstract conceptualizations; subsequent gameplay attempts operationalize active experimentation.

Cognitive Load Theory addresses how instructional design manages working memory limitations and prevents cognitive overload. Game-based environments can strategically reduce extraneous cognitive load (irrelevant perceptual or cognitive demands) while maintaining germane cognitive load (cognitively demanding elements essential to learning objectives). Mayer's multimedia learning principles extend this framework, specifying design approaches (modality principle, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity) that optimize information processing across multiple representational modalities.

+++ Subsection 2.2.2: Game Design and Educational Effectiveness +++

The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design (OBED) framework, articulated by Sison et al., provides systematic methodology for aligning game mechanics with pedagogical objectives. OBED specifies that learning outcomes must be embedded as intrinsic game progression requirements: players advance through game challenges by demonstrating competency in specified learning outcomes rather than through external motivation systems (points, badges, leaderboards). This approach ensures mechanical-pedagogical alignment while maintaining intrinsic motivation.

Clark, Tanner-Smith, and Killingsworth's meta-analysis of digital games and learning (2016) identified modest but significant effect sizes (d = 0.33) when examining game-based versus traditional instruction outcomes. Critically, effect sizes varied substantially based on measurement type: achievement outcomes demonstrated smaller effects (d = 0.26) compared to engagement outcomes (d = 0.55) or transfer behaviors (d = 0.42). This pattern suggests game-based interventions optimally impact engagement and transfer rather than direct achievement gains.

Wouters et al.'s meta-analysis of serious games noted comparable findings: games demonstrated small-to-moderate effects on learning outcomes (d = 0.21) but substantially larger effects on motivation (d = 0.55). These patterns suggest that game-based interventions' primary learning mechanism operates through motivation and engagement amplification rather than direct knowledge transmission.

+++ Subsection 2.2.3: Game-Based Learning in Science Education +++

Science education research documents persistent student misconceptions regarding abstract scientific phenomena, particularly microscopic-level concepts. Phase changes represent exemplary challenge: students develop models wherein liquid water "disappears" during evaporation rather than transforms to vapor, or they confuse heating with temperature increase (failing to understand latent heat effects during phase transitions). Traditional didactic instruction—verbal explanation, still diagrams, textbook descriptions—provides limited access to microscopic phenomena, constraining students' opportunity to develop robust mental models aligned with scientific understanding.

Visualization approaches and interactive representations substantially improve students' conceptual understanding of microscopic phenomena. Adadan's research on multiple representations demonstrated that Grade 11 students exposed to multiple representational formats (macroscopic observations, diagrammatic representations, particle-level animations, and interactive simulations) developed more robust understanding of matter's particulate nature compared to students receiving traditional instruction.

de Jong and van Joolingen documented that computer simulations enabling direct manipulation of variables and observation of consequence patterns significantly enhance students' discovery learning of scientific concepts. The combination of hypothesis formation, experimental manipulation, and consequence observation creates powerful learning cycles exceeding passive observation or demonstration.

Smith and Villarreal's research specifically examined animations depicting phase-change phenomena, documenting that dynamic visualizations significantly improved students' recognition of misconceptions and supported development of scientifically accurate models. The temporal continuity of animation—showing process progression rather than static endpoint representations—proved particularly valuable for phase-change understanding.

--- Section 2.3: Educational Game Design Frameworks ---

+++ Subsection 2.3.1: Multimedia Learning and Cognitive Load +++

Mayer's multimedia learning principles provide evidence-based guidance for designing multi-modal educational content:

The Modality Principle specifies that learning improves when information is presented through multiple modalities (visual and auditory) rather than single-modality (visual text) representations. Game-based environments naturally incorporate diverse modalities: visual representations, auditory cues, kinesthetic manipulation affordances, and narrative contextualization.

The Spatial Contiguity Principle indicates that related information should be proximate in space—printed words should appear adjacent to corresponding diagrams; on-screen labels should be positioned near relevant visual elements. Game interfaces inherently manage spatial relationships; responsive interface design ensures that interaction affordances appear proximate to corresponding visual elements.

The Temporal Contiguity Principle specifies that related information should be temporally proximate—narration should correspond with animation timing; dialogue should align with relevant on-screen actions. Games implement temporal contiguity through synchronized animation, audio timing, and event sequencing.

The Personalization Principle suggests that learning improves when instructional materials are presented in conversational rather than formal language. Game narrative conventions—character-driven dialogue, first-person or second-person address—naturally instantiate personalization.

+++ Subsection 2.3.2: Motivation and Engagement +++

Self-Determination Theory provides framework for understanding intrinsic motivation through three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy (perceived control over one's actions), competence (sense of capability and mastery), and relatedness (sense of social connection). Game-based environments optimally satisfy all three: gameplay affords player choice and agency (autonomy); progressive challenge difficulty and achievement feedback scaffold mastery perception (competence); multiplayer or social game contexts facilitate relatedness.

Plass, Homer, and Kinzer's synthesis of game-based learning research identified engagement as the primary mechanism through which games generate learning benefits. Engagement encompasses attention maintenance, emotional investment, and sustained motivation. Intrinsic motivation—learning pursued for its own inherent satisfaction rather than external rewards—proves more robust and transferable than motivation sustained through external incentives. Games leverage narrative immersion, challenge-skill balance, and achievement recognition to sustain intrinsic motivation.

+++ Subsection 2.3.3: Taxonomy of Learning Outcomes +++

Anderson and Krathwohl's revised Bloom's taxonomy provides framework for categorizing learning outcomes along cognitive complexity dimensions:

Remember: Retrieving relevant knowledge
Understand: Constructing meaning from instructional materials
Apply: Using information in new situations
Analyze: Breaking content into constituent components and identifying relationships
Evaluate: Making judgments based on criteria
Create: Assembling elements to form new conceptual wholes

Game-based learning environments particularly support higher-order outcomes (analyze, evaluate, create) through iterative problem-solving, hypothesis testing, and consequence-responsive gameplay. Phase-change understanding targets Apply and Understand levels (students should apply phase-change knowledge to predict state transitions and articulate conceptual understanding of particle-level mechanisms).

--- Section 2.4: Assessment of Game-Based Learning ---

+++ Subsection 2.4.1: MEEGA+ Kids Evaluation Framework +++

The MEEGA+ Kids (Model for the Evaluation of Educational Games for Computing Education in Primary School) provides instrument-based evaluation of educational games across multiple dimensions: usability (interface intuitiveness, navigational clarity), engagement (attention maintenance, enjoyment, motivation), perceived learning (students' subjective assessment of knowledge gains), and aesthetic quality (visual and audio design). MEEGA+ Kids employs Likert-scale rating items complemented by open-response commentary, enabling quantitative and qualitative evaluation simultaneously.

+++ Subsection 2.4.2: Achievement Assessment Approaches +++

Conventional achievement testing through pre-post designs measures standardized learning outcome attainment. However, this approach captures only one dimension of game-based intervention impact. Thematic analysis of observation data documents engagement trajectories, peer-mediated learning mechanisms, and dispositional shifts toward content areas—learning outcomes unmeasured by traditional testing but potentially consequential for long-term conceptual development.

=== CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY ===

--- Section 3.1: Research Design ---

This study employs mixed-methods design integrating quantitative achievement testing with qualitative engagement analysis. The research follows iterative design framework: initial game development based on pedagogical theory and design principles, formative evaluation incorporating participant feedback during development cycles, design iteration responsive to identified concerns, and summative evaluation assessing efficacy with larger student sample.

+++ Subsection 3.1.1: Participants +++

Formative evaluation (iterative development phase) incorporated 3 Grade 6 students from Inchican Elementary School. These participants provided intensive feedback on usability, engagement, and clarity concerns, guiding design refinement.

Summative evaluation incorporated 18 Grade 6 students from Inchican Elementary School engaged in phase-change instruction within their regular science curriculum. Participants completed pretest assessment, engaged in single-session gameplay (approximately 30 minutes), and completed posttest assessment. Recruitment constraints prevented achievement of target sample size (N=30).

+++ Subsection 3.1.2: Setting +++

Research occurred in authentic school context: Inchican Elementary School, a public school in the Philippines serving students from grades 1-6. Evaluation sessions occurred in school settings utilizing existing student devices where available, supplemented with researcher-provided devices where institutional equipment was unavailable.

--- Section 3.2: Instrumentation ---

+++ Subsection 3.2.1: Achievement Assessment +++

Summative evaluation utilized existing school-administered science assessments examining three learning outcome categories:

Learning Outcome 1 (LO1): Describe changes of state—Students demonstrate ability to name and describe melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, and deposition processes.

Learning Outcome 2 (LO2): Identify factors affecting phase changes—Students identify temperature and pressure effects on phase transitions and predict directional state changes.

Learning Outcome 3 (LO3): Explain particle behavior during phase transitions—Students articulate microscopic-level explanations of particle arrangement, motion, and bonding changes during phase transitions.

Pretest administration occurred prior to gameplay; posttest administration followed single-session gameplay intervention.

+++ Subsection 3.2.2: Usability and Engagement Assessment +++

Formative evaluation employed MEEGA+ Kids instrument, comprising Likert-scale ratings (1=Strongly Disagree to 5=Strongly Agree) across multiple dimensions: usability (control intuitiveness, instructional clarity, objective identification, navigation functionality), engagement (enjoyment, attention maintenance, affective response), perceived learning (subjective assessment of learning effectiveness), and aesthetic quality (visual design appeal, audio quality, difficulty appropriateness).

+++ Subsection 3.2.3: Qualitative Data Collection +++

Researcher field notes document observational data throughout formative and summative evaluation sessions: participant behavioral responses, engagement indicators, peer interaction patterns, frustration episodes, and qualitative commentary. Field notes were systematically coded using thematic analysis methodology, identifying conceptual categories representing behavioral and affective states.

--- Section 3.3: Data Analysis Methodology ---

+++ Subsection 3.3.1: Quantitative Analysis +++

Pretest-posttest achievement scores were analyzed using Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test, a non-parametric paired-samples comparison test appropriate for small samples or non-normally distributed data. Prior normality assessment employed Shapiro-Wilk test; analyses employed non-parametric methodology given identified non-normality across learning outcome categories.

+++ Subsection 3.3.2: Qualitative Analysis +++

Researcher field notes were systematically coded using thematic analysis methodology. Observational data were organized into conceptual categories representing participant behavioral and affective states. Coding identified patterns of engagement trajectories, peer interaction dynamics, and dispositional responses. The analytical framework organized observations into developmental stages representing progression through gameplay sessions.

--- Section 3.4: Research Ethics and Limitations ---

This research followed institutional ethical guidelines with informed consent from school administrators and student assent from participants. Data collection procedures prioritized minimal disruption to regular academic activities and provided students with engaging, non-threatening research participation experiences.

Methodological limitations include small sample size, single-session intervention design, and single-site implementation. These constraints limit statistical power and generalizability; findings are presented as illustrative of implementation feasibility and design effectiveness within the specific context rather than as broadly generalizable outcomes.

The study emphasizes the importance of aligning game mechanics with pedagogical frameworks while maintaining engagement through iterative user feedback and accessibility considerations suitable for the target demographic.

Following the Outcomes-Based Edugame Design framework, the platformer serves as the narrative and mechanical premise, establishing a coherent game world that requires spatial navigation from an entry point to an exit objective. This design pattern is grounded in established game design principles exemplified by seminal titles such as Super Mario Bros, wherein level progression follows a directional narrative structure—typically left-to-right progression culminating in a goal object or adversary encounter.

Environmental hazards, specifically pit obstacles, are integrated into multiple levels to introduce challenge and risk progression. +++ Subsection 4.4.2: Higher Order Game +++

The higher-order game mechanics are operationalized through the Particle Lattice System—an internally developed subsystem that generates particle configuration diagrams representing the microscopic structural arrangement of solids, liquids, and gases. This system was engineered with extensibility as a primary design principle to accommodate multiple phases, materials, and parameter presets configured by developers. This architecture enables potential implementations of diverse materials beyond water—the conventional exemplar in phase-change pedagogy—such as steel or clay. However, implementation scope was constrained by development timeline limitations.

The system implements parametric configurability through developer-accessible parameters that can be set and persisted for material simulation. Key configurable properties include particle attributes (size, initial temperature, quantity, spatial arrangement) and intermolecular bond characteristics (length, stiffness, elasticity). This parametric foundation provides pedagogical extensibility while maintaining implementational efficiency.

[FIGURE: LatticeSettings.png - Particle Lattice System configurable parameters]

Within Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter, the Particle Lattice System generates interactive minigame instances that players encounter repeatedly throughout level progression. When players encounter environmental obstacles that impede forward progress—such as pit hazards, ice blocks, or water barriers—an interaction affordance (visual button prompt) appears on-screen, enabling players to engage the minigame interface associated with that obstacle.

[FIGURE: InteractionButton.png - Interaction Button Appears]

The specific minigame instantiation depends on the state of matter corresponding to the obstacle's material. States of matter are operationalized with distinct gameplay properties: solids exhibit collision properties and obstruct player movement; liquids permit player traversal but apply buoyant force opposing gravity; gases present no collision properties but enable novel interaction mechanics.

Players manipulate particles through direct interaction (dragging), experiencing firsthand the elastic, rigid, or compliant properties of different matter states through haptic feedback and visual responsivity. Lattice visualizations are designed to correspond with canonical pedagogical diagrams found in standard science curricula, ensuring cognitive alignment with existing mental models from classroom instruction. An on-screen hint provides explicit state-of-matter identification.

+++ Subsection 4.4.3: Synthesis of Lower and Higher Order Games +++

Challenge scenarios are introduced through explicit narrative dialogue that specifies the required phase transition but withholds procedural methodology. Players are left to independently discover the application mechanics of specified phase transitions, fostering autonomous problem-solving and reinforcing the causal relationship between higher-order game actions and lower-order environmental state changes.

[FIGURE: ExplicitInstruction.png - Player being told to MELT the ice]

This pedagogical coupling of lower and higher-order game systems positions players in continuous active experimentation cycles. The game environment facilitates iterative hypothesis testing regarding phase-transition mechanisms and effects. This cyclical gameplay pattern—characteristic of game-loop design—enables players to internalize conceptual models through repeated experimentation and observation. The expectation is that through sustained gameplay iteration, players will develop robust mental models of matter state structures, thermodynamic mechanisms, and phase-transition flowcharts that transfer to real-world contexts.

[FIGURE: DanielCook.png - Daniel Cook's Game Loop Diagram]

User interface evolution involved iterative simplification to minimize cognitive load. Early prototypes employed color-coded particles, colored bond visualizations, dynamic object rotation, and extensive on-screen text—design choices that generated player confusion and decision paralysis. The final interface implementation prioritizes essential information through: (1) unadorned particle lattice with kinetic energy representation (particle oscillation intensity correlates with thermal energy; bond rigidity visually decreases with entropy), (2) spellbook sprite icons replacing descriptive text labels, (3) explicit phase-transition labels (e.g., "Melting: Solid to Liquid"), and (4) confirmation/cancellation affordances enabling committed action.

--- Section 4.6: Other Systems ---

+++ Subsection 4.6.1: Mini Dialogue System +++

The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design framework specifies narrative contextualization as essential to meaningful gameplay. Effective narrative requires answering fundamental questions: Why is the player engaging this experience? Who does the player inhabit? What are the player's objectives within a relatable context? Balancing narrative immersion against player autonomy presented design tension: excessive exposition overwhelms players and undermines agency; insufficient context obscures purpose and reduces engagement.

This design challenge yielded the Mini Dialogue System—a lightweight, unobtrusive narrative delivery mechanism. The nomenclature reflects both implementational parsimony (minimal code complexity) and experiential design (minimal screen real estate consumption). The system presents context-sensitive prompts accessible via screen-top interaction points. Prompts are triggered upon level initiation or upon entering specific spatial regions, delivering explicit gameplay instructions, critical information, or narrative dialogue without disrupting primary gameplay flow.

[FIGURE: DialogueSample.png - Sample Dialogue]

Internationalization support was initially conceptualized to include Tagalog language options, anticipated to provide improved accessibility for target demographic members more fluent in the native Philippine language. Implementation was deferred due to development timeline constraints. Additionally, extensive terminology from the learning competency ("sublimation," "deposition," "bonding") and platform-specific action vocabulary ("tap," "swipe," "toggle") resisted direct translation without semantic loss, potentially impairing comprehension for Tagalog-preferring learners. These linguistic and practical constraints motivated deferral of localization to post-release development phases.

=== CHAPTER 5: DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION ISSUES ===

--- Section 5.1: Lower Order Mechanics ---

Platformer design presented significant pedagogical and technical challenges absent explicit baseline data regarding the target demographic's motor skills, platform familiarity, and gaming experience. Advisory consultation emphasized critical design constraints: avoid overestimating player capabilities and avoid designing the game experience around developer competencies rather than target learner competencies. This directive was validated repeatedly during iterative development reviews: the principal investigator demonstrated difficulty executing implemented platformer mechanics (jumping timing, coyote time implementations, quick-jump sequences), raising critical concerns about appropriateness for 11–13 year-old players.

This difficulty generated design tension: excessively demanding platformer mechanics risked frustrating players and preventing progression; overly simplistic mechanics risked disengagement through insufficient challenge. The resolution leveraged asymmetric difficulty distribution: platformer mechanics were intentionally constrained to facilitate broad accessibility, while the coupled higher-order minigame system provided substantial cognitive challenge. This strategy positioned pit obstacles as engagement mechanics introducing excitement and perceived consequence without requiring advanced motor control execution.

Level design clarity received iterative refinement to enable rapid player assessment of obstacle traversability. Formative playtesting observations at Inchican Elementary School documented recurring decision-making patterns:

- "Can I make this jump?" (Indigenous language: "Kaya ko ba talunin ito?")
- "Can I bruteforce my way up this wall?" (Indigenous language: "Kaya ko tong akyatin eh")
- "I don't think I have to melt this block" (Indigenous language: "Hindi ko na ata kailangan gawin ito eh" [referring to minigame requirement])

These observations informed iterative adjustments to visual affordances, collision boundaries, and spatial layout clarity. Beyond challenge calibration, level progression incorporated narrative progression through environmental design. Early iterations featured minimal vertical variation and spatially limited levels with entry and exit points at equivalent elevations. Subsequent iterations introduced graduated vertical complexity, establishing a coherent spatial narrative of ascending a snow-covered mountain toward a temple structure. Aesthetic differentiation through tileset variation (mountain ice and snow textures contrasting with temple brick and wood materials) reinforced this narrative progression while maintaining visual clarity and platform affordance legibility.

--- Section 5.2: Higher Order Mechanics ---

Implementation of the Higher Order Game system presented multifaceted technical and pedagogical design challenges:

+++ Subsection 5.2.1: Visual Clarity +++

A foundational design question centered on effective visualization of the Particle Lattice System to establish learner connection between minigame representations and established pedagogical diagrams of particulate matter structure. The challenge was inherently dual-natured: communicating temperature and bond stiffness—variables critically distinguishing rigid solid particle packing from loosely bonded liquid configurations—without resorting to explicit numerical or textual display that would negatively impact user experience for the target demographic.

Early iterations employed color-coded particle and bond visualizations. However, advisory review identified critical pedagogical risk: chromatic variation could be misinterpreted as signifying chemical change (material transformation) rather than physical state transition, introducing substantive misconception contrary to learning objectives.

[FIGURE: ChangingColors.png - Orange originally signified "Hot" particles while Blue meant "Cold" particles]

Resolution was achieved through kinetic visualization. Visual feedback was repositioned from chromatic variation to motion-based representation: particle oscillation intensity correlates with thermal energy (hotter particles exhibit greater vibration amplitude); bond visual properties respond to stiffness reduction (bonds become progressively thinner and longer as molecular motion increases, eventually disappearing to represent unbonded free-particle gas configurations). This kinetic encoding communicates thermodynamic state changes through observable motion rather than color, avoiding misconception regarding chemical transformation.

Supplementary visual effects were incorporated: spellbook interaction generates symbolic particle effects utilizing the originally intended color associations in effect visualizations rather than in the lattice representation itself. This design preserves color-coding associativity (red→heat, blue→cold, green→bonding) while preventing misinterpretation of particle lattice representations.

+++ Subsection 5.2.2: Controls +++

Control scheme design leveraged platform-specific affordances of mobile touchscreen devices. Interactive methodology relies on direct screen contact ("touch and rub" gestural interaction), permitting unrestricted player manipulation of particle lattice configurations. This approach prioritizes expressive freedom and discovery-based interaction over prescriptive procedural sequences.

Supporting interface controls facilitate error recovery and iterative exploration: Confirm, Cancel, and Reset buttons enable player correction of unintended lattice modifications before commitment to phase transition and return to lower-order gameplay. This redundancy was necessitated by complexities inherent in Unity's built-in Rigidbody physics engine integration, which introduced occasional unintended simulation artifacts or configurations requiring player correction.

+++ Subsection 5.2.3: Coupling of Lower and Higher-Order Systems +++

Mechanically coupling the platformer-based lower-order game to the minigame-based higher-order game required designing environmental challenges that strategically necessitated creative problem-solving and critical thinking. Formative playtesting revealed significant limitation in early implementations: minigame content was constrained to melting ice blocks, resulting in repetitive gameplay lacking variety and sustained engagement.

Resolution involved systematic brainstorming and negative testing protocols (multiple weeks of intentional edge-case identification and failure scenario validation) to ensure players encountered diverse phase-change applications without overwhelming cognitive load. Progressive spellbook introduction was carefully sequenced to introduce new mechanics and phase transitions gradually while maintaining engagement and perceived challenge. The scenario mapping detailed in Table 4.2 (presented in Section 4.4.2) represents the culmination of this design iteration: six distinct gameplay scenarios systematically require player application of all six phase transitions, ensuring comprehensive conceptual engagement while maintaining logical progression and narrative coherence.

--- Section 5.3: Issues Upon Deployment ---

Deployment challenges were predominantly logistical rather than technical. Unforeseen institutional constraints affected playtest execution: device availability fluctuations and variable internet infrastructure at the partner institution (Inchican Elementary School) created implementation uncertainties. These challenges were prospectively mitigated through multi-platform deployment strategy: development of a web-based build enabled direct browser-based gameplay without requiring device-level software installation. This approach substantially reduced setup time overhead during limited available evaluation periods.

Technical issues arising during summative evaluation were minimal and non-blocking. Two technical artifacts were documented: visual rendering anomalies in Level 7 and a movement mechanics bug preventing players from achieving standard jump height following water-based minigame completion and exit. Neither issue prevented level progression or experiment completion, as per-level restart functionality was provided as error recovery mechanism.

The strategic deployment approach—leveraging web-based delivery to address logistical constraints and incorporating per-level recovery mechanics to handle edge-case technical issues—enabled successful summative evaluation despite infrastructural constraints typical of resource-limited educational institutions in the Philippine context.

+++ Subsection 6.1.2: MEEGA+ Kids Results +++

The MEEGA+ Kids evaluation instrument was administered during the formative evaluation phase to assess usability, engagement, and perceived pedagogical effectiveness. Due to recruitment constraints, a small sample of three Grade 6 students participated, providing quantitative Likert-scale ratings and qualitative commentary regarding gameplay experience. While the limited sample size constrains generalizability, the intensity of formative evaluation protocol prioritized depth of iterative feedback: individual participant concerns and reported difficulties were systematically amplified within development cycles as critical signals warranting immediate remediation, ensuring that even singular or low-frequency complaints triggered design investigation and refinement.

Usability and Interface Design: The game received generally favorable evaluations across interface accessibility dimensions. Participants demonstrated strong agreement regarding control intuitiveness and in-game instructional clarity. Most respondents successfully identified gameplay objectives; however, one participant's neutral rating on objective clarity signaled minor inconsistencies in goal-state communication, prompting interface refinement to enhance affordance legibility. Navigation functionality between menus and level progression received mixed ratings (neutral to strongly agree), indicating adequate technical functionality with remaining potential for improved interface design intuitiveness.

Engagement and Affective Response: The game demonstrated robust performance across engagement metrics. All participants reported enjoyment and expressed willingness for continued gameplay. Consistent attention maintenance was documented across responses (agree to strongly agree range), complemented by highly favorable ratings of presentation elements (graphics, audio). This aesthetic-affective engagement appeared to successfully motivate challenge-completion behavior, suggesting effective integration of motivational design principles.

Perceived Learning Effectiveness: All participants reported agreement or strong agreement that gameplay enhanced phase-change conceptual understanding. Specifically, visual representations of microscopic particle behavior during phase transitions received particular commendation as conceptually clarifying. These findings suggest alignment with multimedia learning theory and constructivist epistemology, wherein visualization and interactive manipulation scaffold knowledge construction of abstract concepts.

Visual Design and Difficulty Calibration: Graphics and animation sequences received favorable aesthetic evaluations. Difficulty assessment indicated generally appropriate challenge calibration; however, neutral responses regarding ice-interaction mechanics signaled specific clarity deficits requiring targeted refinement. These singular concerns, despite small sample size, were internally prioritized as critical design signals necessitating investigation and revision.

Overall Assessment: Formative evaluation yielded consistently high satisfaction ratings across participants. The game successfully operationalized core design objectives: creating an engaging, accessible interface supporting both enjoyment and conceptual learning. However, concentrated attention to minority concerns (single participant's objective-clarity rating, neutral difficulty responses) guided subsequent development cycles toward enhanced clarity and accessibility. This amplification of small-sample feedback signals reflects a deliberate design philosophy treating each participant comment as potentially consequential to overall user experience optimization, thereby compensating for limited sample generalizability through intensive iterative refinement.

+++ Subsection 6.1.3: In-Game Analysis +++

--- Section 6.2: Thematic Analysis ---

Researcher field notes from formative and summative playtesting sessions were analyzed using thematic coding methodology. Observational data were systematically coded and organized into conceptual categories representing distinct participant behavioral and affective states. Fifteen thematic concepts were identified:

Disinterested: Low-energy participant responses (codes: Late, Busy, No commitment)
Interested: High-engagement responses (codes: Eager, Helping, Talkative, Friends)
Converted: Transition from disinterest to interest (codes: Change, Peer Pressure, Chance)
Curious: Frequent researcher interaction (codes: Asking, Connection, Wonder)
Bored: External distraction focus (codes: TikTok, Dismissal, Snacks)
Mentors: Peer-guidance behavior (codes: Loud, Leader, Popular)
Mentees: Active listening to mentors (codes: Quiet, Listens, Understanding)
Annoyed: Control/performance frustration (codes: Complaints, Diminishing, Cussing)
Invested: Purpose inquiry and engagement (codes: Aspirant, Willing, Inspired)
Bruteforcer: Non-standard progression attempts (codes: Rebellious, Rush, Hardheaded)
Competitive: Achievement-oriented behavior (codes: Boaster, Hyper, Gamer)
Satisfied: Positive session conclusion (codes: Compliments, Appreciation, Happy)
Frustrated: Negative session conclusion (codes: Mistakes, Takebacks, Redo)
Relieved: Eager session termination (codes: Exhausted, Dismay, Obligation)
Wanting: Future interest inquiry (codes: Future, Subscription, Fulfillment)

[FIGURE: ThematicAnalysis.png - Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter Edugame Framework]

The analytical framework identified five progressive behavioral stages observed during playtesting. Stage 1 (Initial Reception) characterized participants' first impressions upon arrival, dichotomously categorized as Interested or Disinterested based on observable engagement signals (body language, verbal expression, facial demeanor). Contextual factors were significant: as evaluation occurred during academic transition to summer vacation, many participants exhibited preoccupation with concurrent graduation activities, manifesting as evident disinterest despite research participation obligations.

Stage 2 (Session Initiation) occurred as formal procedures commenced. Participants transitioned along behavioral trajectories: Disinterested individuals intensified into Bored states (external focus distraction), while Interested participants differentiated into Curious (researcher-interactive) or Converted (attitudinal shift) categories. Notable observation: one participant explicitly articulated resistance, claiming the research session infringed upon personal time priorities.

Stage 3 (Gameplay Exposure) emerged following game deployment with minimal social restriction. Participants engaged in spontaneous peer collaboration and mentoring relationships. Mentor-Mentee dyads self-organized to facilitate progression; notably, some mentors explicitly verbalized pedagogical reinforcement, audibly reminding peers of taught phase-change concepts while encouraging strategic problem-solving. This stage demonstrated organic peer learning mechanisms and affective engagement beyond structured instruction.

Stage 4 (Sustained Gameplay) characterized participants' post-introductory engagement with advanced game content. Focus shifted from social to individual gameplay. Differentiated behavioral manifestations emerged: Annoyed states reflected frustration with control mechanics or device limitations; Invested states reflected participants (particularly those completing full gameplay) initiating researcher dialogue regarding game mechanics and design intent; Bruteforcers attempted unconventional progression strategies to accelerate completion; Competitive participants repeatedly referenced completion status and engaged mentoring behavior while maintaining achievement-oriented framing.

Stage 5 (Session Conclusion) captured exit-state observations. Positive termination states included Satisfied participants (expressing appreciation and contentment) and Wanting participants (articulating interest in future game development and research continuation). Negative exit states included Frustrated participants (expressing complaints regarding assessment or gameplay difficulty) and Relieved participants (displaying relief at session termination, occasionally articulating fatigue or interpersonal frustration).

The thematic framework reveals a complex ecology of engagement factors operating during gameplay assessment. Beyond quantitative performance metrics, observable affective and social dimensions—including peer mentoring activation, intrinsic motivation dynamics, competitive engagement, and frustration tolerance—structured the playtesting experience. Notably, peer-mediated learning (mentoring relationships spontaneously emerging during gameplay) appeared to mobilize pedagogical engagement independent of formal instruction. The framework suggests that summative assessment data (pretest-posttest scores) may substantially underestimate intervention impact, as affective engagement, social learning dynamics, and dispositional shifts toward scientific content are undetected by conventional achievement testing. The heterogeneity of exit states (ranging from deeply Wanting to Relief-motivated departure) indicates differential intervention responsiveness across participant subgroups, suggesting that aggregate quantitative findings mask meaningful subgroup variation in learning trajectories and engagement sustainability.

--- Section 6.3: Discussion of Findings ---

The quantitative and qualitative findings present a nuanced narrative regarding Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter's pedagogical efficacy. The absence of statistically significant improvement in pretest-posttest achievement metrics stands in apparent tension with robust evidence of affective engagement, peer-mediated learning activation, and sustained motivation documented through thematic analysis.

Several explanatory factors merit consideration. First, the brief intervention window (single-session gameplay exposure) may be insufficient for consolidating conceptual understanding measurable through standardized assessment. Learning outcome consolidation—particularly for abstract scientific constructs such as microscopic particle behavior during phase transitions—typically requires distributed practice across multiple exposures. A single thirty-minute gameplay session, while engaging and affectively positive, likely represents insufficient dosage for transforming fundamental conceptual understanding as captured by criterion-referenced assessments.

Second, temporal proximity between intervention and posttest administration may contribute to observed null findings. The research timeline positioned the summative evaluation during academic transition period (summer vacation) following students' completion of phase-change instruction during regular curriculum delivery. This temporal configuration means posttest administration occurred after extended interval from initial instruction, during which consolidation and retention may have naturally degraded across both control and intervention conditions. The ceiling effects observed in certain learning outcome categories (particularly LO1 with median floor of 2–3 points) suggest potential test-design limitations: instruments may lack discriminative sensitivity in the target performance range, producing compressed score distributions limiting statistical power.

Third, and significantly, the thematic framework reveals that quantitative achievement metrics captured through conventional testing may be fundamentally misaligned with the mechanisms through which game-based interventions generate educational value. The documented emergence of peer-mentoring relationships, spontaneous pedagogical reinforcement ("remember the phase changes you learned"), and sustained curiosity regarding game mechanics and underlying scientific principles suggest that the intervention catalyzed dispositional and social-cognitive shifts—increased curiosity, peer-supported inquiry, recognition of personal agency in scientific problem-solving—that precede and scaffold conventional conceptual achievement. Achievement tests administered immediately post-intervention may not yet reflect consolidation of these prerequisite dispositional shifts into measurable concept mastery.

Furthermore, the heterogeneity of engagement trajectories observed through thematic analysis indicates that intervention impact varies substantially across participant subgroups. Participants characterized as Wanting or Invested demonstrated explicit interest in continued engagement and deeper scientific exploration, suggesting intrinsic motivation amplification. Conversely, Relieved or Frustrated participants, while potentially experiencing affective engagement during gameplay, exited with negative dispositional orientations. Aggregate quantitative analysis obscures this subgroup heterogeneity: a meaningful subset of participants may have experienced substantial motivational or conceptual gain, while others exhibited minimal response, with aggregate metrics reflecting minimal net change.

Regarding the comparison with established literature: previous game-based learning meta-analyses frequently report modest effect sizes, particularly when measurements emphasize standardized achievement outcomes rather than motivation, engagement, or transfer behaviors. The present findings align with this literature pattern: affective and social learning outcomes are prominent, while conventional achievement metrics show null effects. This pattern suggests that game-based interventions may optimally function within curricular frameworks that explicitly value and assess engagement, intrinsic motivation, and scientific curiosity alongside traditional achievement metrics.

The technical implementation challenges identified in Chapter 5—particularly control mechanics and device limitations that generated Annoyed states—may have suppressed achievement gains by diverting cognitive resources toward frustration management rather than conceptual consolidation. Future iterations incorporating more robust mobile interface design and streamlined control mechanics may reduce extraneous cognitive load, potentially enabling achievement improvements.

--- Section 6.4: Conclusion ---

This study examined the design, development, and evaluation of Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter, a 2D educational platformer game designed to reinforce Grade 6 students' understanding of phase changes (melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation) within the MATATAG science curriculum. The intervention integrated lower-order platformer mechanics with higher-order particle lattice manipulation systems, leveraging established pedagogical frameworks (Outcomes-Based Edugame Design) to align gameplay progression with conceptual mastery. Development incorporated iterative evaluation and deployment across multiple platforms to address accessibility constraints in resource-limited educational contexts.

Summative evaluation findings reveal a complex efficacy profile. Quantitative pretest-posttest analysis using Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests across three learning outcome categories and composite achievement scores yielded no statistically significant improvements (all p > 0.05), indicating that the intervention, as currently implemented, was insufficient to produce measurable test-score gains within the specified evaluation timeframe. However, this quantitative null finding stands in marked contrast to substantive qualitative evidence: MEEGA+ Kids evaluation data and thematic analysis of researcher field notes documented robust affective engagement, high perceived learning support, spontaneous peer-mentoring relationships, and predominantly positive dispositional exit states. Learners consistently reported that the game was enjoyable, visually appealing, and effective in illustrating phase-change concepts through interactive and visual elements.

This discrepancy between perceived learning effectiveness and standardized achievement metrics may be attributed to several interrelated factors: (1) limited gameplay exposure duration (single thirty-minute session providing insufficient consolidation dosage for complex abstract concepts), (2) small sample size constraining statistical power and generalizability, (3) temporal assessment issues (posttest administration during summer vacation interval following extended instructional gap), and (4) assessment design limitations (ceiling/floor effects reducing discriminative sensitivity). Additionally, specific gameplay mechanics—particularly ice-interaction implementations—introduced clarity challenges that diverted cognitive resources from conceptual consolidation, suppressing achievement gains.

Despite null quantitative findings, the study demonstrates critical contributions to understanding game-based learning implementation in resource-constrained contexts. First, it validates technical and instructional feasibility: educationally aligned games addressing abstract scientific content can be developed with finite resources and deployed effectively in authentic school settings despite infrastructural limitations. Second, it illustrates the critical importance of assessment pluralism: conventional achievement testing alone substantially underrepresents learning mechanisms (peer-supported inquiry, dispositional shifts toward scientific curiosity, intrinsic motivation amplification) that game-based interventions mobilize. Third, it identifies specific pedagogical mechanisms (peer-mediated learning, narrative scaffolding, repeated exposure to particle-level representations) through which gameplay supports science learning.

In conclusion, Wiz's Journey: Mastering the States of Matter demonstrates strong potential as a supplementary learning tool for catalyzing engagement, curiosity, and peer-supported inquiry regarding abstract scientific content. While immediate achievement gains were not measured, the intervention catalyzed meaningful dispositional and social-cognitive shifts that scaffold long-term conceptual development. The study underscores the importance of balancing gameplay design accessibility, instructional clarity, robust assessment alignment, and extended implementation duration in developing effective educational games. Further refinement addressing identified clarity concerns and extended intervention exposure across multiple sessions would be necessary to achieve measurable improvements in standardized achievement outcomes.

--- Section 6.5: Recommendations ---

Based on findings and limitations identified throughout this research, the following recommendations are proposed to guide future development and evaluation of educational games targeting scientific concept mastery.

Extended Intervention Duration: Future implementations should prioritize extended exposure through multiple gameplay sessions rather than single-session protocols. Learning complex abstract scientific constructs—particularly those requiring integration of microscopic conceptual models with macroscopic observational phenomena—typically necessitates distributed practice across repeated interactions. Multi-session interventions spanning weeks or months would permit knowledge consolidation and retention assessment, enabling detection of learning gains that single-session designs fail to capture.

Mechanical Clarity Refinement: Priority should be assigned to eliminating identified ambiguities in gameplay mechanics, particularly ice-interaction implementations and state-transition visualizations. Iterative usability testing with target demographic members, enhanced visual feedback systems, progressive disclosure of instructional complexity through guided tutorials, and strategic scaffolding hierarchies would reduce cognitive friction and improve conceptual-mechanical integration.

Sample Expansion and Consistency: Future studies should recruit larger, homogeneous samples of Grade 6 students currently engaged in phase-change instruction within MATATAG curricula. Sample sizes of N ≥ 50–100 with consistent participant characteristics would substantially improve statistical power, internal validity, and findings generalizability across educational contexts.

Experimental Design Enhancement: Implementation of more rigorous experimental designs incorporating control or comparison conditions would enable causal attribution of observed effects. Quasi-experimental designs with matched comparison groups, delayed posttest assessment measuring retention, and longitudinal follow-up examining transfer to new problem contexts would provide stronger evidence of intervention efficacy.

Adaptive Scaffolding Systems: Integration of dynamic difficulty adjustment and personalized scaffolding mechanisms based on real-time performance assessment would enable the game to support learners across heterogeneous skill levels and prior knowledge distributions. Adaptive systems capable of detecting conceptual gaps and providing targeted instructional support would optimize learning efficiency.

In-Game Analytics and Behavioral Tracking: Implementation of comprehensive telemetry systems capturing detailed gameplay data—including time-on-task metrics, attempt frequencies, progression velocities, interaction sequences, mechanic utilization patterns, and error recovery behaviors—would enable fine-grained analysis of how specific design features influence learning processes and outcomes. Such data would identify engagement mechanisms and design bottlenecks warranting refinement.

Assessment Pluralism: Future research should move beyond exclusive reliance on standardized pre-post achievement testing by integrating rich qualitative methodologies: semi-structured interviews exploring conceptual understanding development, think-aloud protocols during gameplay documenting real-time reasoning processes, classroom observation assessing transfer to standard science instruction contexts, and dispositional assessments measuring sustained curiosity and scientific agency. Such pluralistic approaches would capture the multidimensional learning outcomes that game-based interventions generate.

Longitudinal Impact Studies: Extended follow-up investigations tracking participants over months or years post-intervention would assess whether game-catalyzed engagement and dispositional shifts translate into sustained conceptual understanding, improved science course achievement, and increased science career interest—outcomes exceeding immediate test-score improvements but reflecting the transformative potential of engagement-centered pedagogies.
