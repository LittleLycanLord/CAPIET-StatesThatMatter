\subsection{Current Curriculum of Philippines Education}

The Philippines' education system has undergone significant institutional changes in recent years, with the K-12 program serving as the current standardized framework. The K-12 program was implemented to align the Philippine curriculum with international standards and improve compatibility with global education systems \cite{Abragan2022}. However, recent critical assessments have revealed substantial gaps between intended learning outcomes and actual student performance in classroom settings. Research indicates that discrepancies emerge from multiple factors, including curriculum design, instructional delivery approaches, and alignment of pedagogical objectives with assessment practices \cite{Descartin2023}.

A significant challenge identified by Kilag et al. (2024) is curriculum overload---the K-12 program's extensive content requirements leave insufficient time and space for meaningful student-centered learning \cite{Kilag2024}. In response to disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and the shift to remote instruction, the Department of Education introduced the Most Essential Learning Competencies (MELC) framework \cite{Marasigan2021}. The MELC was designed to distill curricula to their core components, ensuring that the most critical competencies were prioritized during instruction \cite{Zalun2023, Gallego2024}. Despite these intentions, implementation has revealed persistent challenges---both new issues emerging from MELC adoption and enduring problems that preceded it \cite{Angay2023, Gallego2024}.

% ============================================================================
% Subsection 2.1.2: Status of the Philippine Education
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Status of the Philippine Education}

The broader educational landscape in the Philippines reflects substantial variation in system readiness and implementation capacity across regions and school types. Urban and metropolitan schools, particularly private institutions, generally demonstrate stronger infrastructure and resource availability, enabling more robust curriculum implementation. In contrast, rural and underserved urban schools face systemic capacity constraints that compromise their ability to deliver the full breadth of competencies outlined in national curriculum frameworks \cite{Kilag2024}.

Teaching force preparedness remains a significant concern. While Filipino teachers demonstrate strong commitment and pedagogical dedication, many lack formal professional development in contemporary instructional methodologies---particularly those integrating technology, differentiated instruction, and competency-based assessment \cite{Abragan2022}. Furthermore, administrative support systems for curriculum implementation vary widely, with limited monitoring and feedback mechanisms in resource-constrained schools to ensure fidelity to curriculum standards \cite{Gallego2024}.

Student learning outcomes in foundational subjects such as science and mathematics have shown modest improvement trends but remain below regional benchmarks. International assessments and national standardized tests indicate persistent achievement gaps correlating with school resource levels and geographic location. These patterns underscore the need for targeted interventions addressing both systemic capacity and localized resource constraints (Descartin et al., 2023).

% Subsection 2.1.3: Challenges of Resource Poor Schools
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Challenges of Resource Poor Schools}

Resource scarcity presents a fundamental barrier to equitable curriculum implementation in Philippine schools. Many schools, particularly in rural and urban marginalized communities, contend with insufficient learning materials, overcrowded classrooms, and limited access to contemporary instructional tools \cite{Kilag2024}. Abragan and Abarcas et al. (2022) documented that teachers in resource-poor schools lack adequate training in technology integration and have minimal access to information and communication technology (ICT) equipment---tools that could enhance student engagement and maintain focus during instruction \cite{Abragan2022}.

A critical gap in science education infrastructure is the scarcity of functional science laboratories in resource-poor schools. Many institutions lack dedicated laboratory facilities, essential equipment (microscopes, measuring apparatus, safety equipment), and consumable materials required for hands-on experimentation in chemistry and physics. This absence forces teachers to rely exclusively on theoretical demonstrations and textbook illustrations, severely limiting students' ability to conduct direct observations and experiential learning in abstract scientific domains such as phase changes and matter properties \cite{Kilag2024}.

Curriculum implementation challenges are further compounded by deficiencies in teacher preparation. Curriculum unpacking---the process by which educators analyze curriculum competencies to derive specific learning objectives, instructional materials, and assessment activities---requires specialized training and pedagogical expertise. Many teachers lack formal guidance in this process, resulting in literal interpretation of competency statements without meaningful contextualization or sequencing \cite{Gallego2024}. This insufficient preparation, combined with limited access to resources, leads to compromised mastery of prerequisite concepts, creating foundational gaps that undermine student performance in higher-order learning objectives \cite{Gabriel2022}.

Research consistently demonstrates that resource-rich schools---particularly private institutions with superior facility access and professional development infrastructure---achieve significantly higher academic outcomes than their resource-poor counterparts \cite{Angay2023}. To address this inequity, scholars have called for expanded provision of learning resources across all school contexts. The present study responds to this imperative by developing an educational platformer game focused on reinforcing Grade 6 competencies related to phase changes and states of matter. By leveraging the wide availability of mobile devices and web-accessible platforms, this game-based intervention can reach students regardless of whether their schools possess traditional science laboratory facilities. The game provides interactive, visual simulations of molecular-level phenomena aligned with MATATAG competencies, enabling students to engage with abstract scientific concepts through accessible gaming technology that requires minimal institutional infrastructure beyond basic internet connectivity.

\subsection{Particle Models and Phase Transitions in Elementary Learning}

Understanding the particulate nature of matter and phase transitions is fundamental to chemistry and science literacy. Samarapungavan, Bryan, Staudt, and colleagues (2023) explored kindergarten and early elementary students' learning of simple particle models to explain the properties and behavior of matter in solid, liquid, and gas states during phase transitions. Their research found that when students are explicitly taught to use particle models as explanatory frameworks, they develop more accurate mental representations of microscopic processes underlying observable macroscopic phenomena. The study emphasizes that science instruction focusing solely on concrete, directly observable phenomena without explicit instruction in particulate models limits students' ability to understand abstract concepts fundamental to chemistry. Research has consistently identified misconceptions about particle behavior in matter as a primary barrier to chemistry understanding \cite{Adadan2013}.

% ============================================================================
% Subsection 2.2.2: Misconceptions in Melting and Freezing Cycles
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Misconceptions in Melting and Freezing Cycles}

A critical challenge in science education is bridging the gap between macroscopic observations and microscopic explanations. Smith and Villarreal (2015) assessed 155 freshman general chemistry students' misconceptions about particle position and arrangement during the melting-freezing cycle using the Melting Cycle Instrument, which employs particulate-level representations \cite{Smith2015}. Their findings revealed that many students maintained inaccurate mental models about how particles behave during phase transitions, particularly regarding particle spacing and motion during state changes. These misconceptions persist even after formal instruction, suggesting the need for innovative pedagogical approaches that make particle-level interactions more salient and comprehensible to learners across educational levels. Research in this area has also documented that students in Korean high schools hold similar conceptions about phase changes and particle motion \cite{Paik2004}.

% ============================================================================
% Subsection 2.2.3: Remediation of Matter Misconceptions Through Knowledge Revision
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Remediation of Matter Misconceptions Through Knowledge Revision}

The persistent nature of matter misconceptions has prompted researchers to investigate interventions designed to remediate student errors. Korur, Korumaz, and Avci (2025) developed the Knowledge Revision Components (KReC) framework, which integrates refutation texts and advanced organizer materials to explicitly address and eliminate misconceptions about matter and physical/chemical changes. Their research demonstrated that systematic, theory-based approaches to misconception remediation significantly improve student conceptual understanding when learners are directly confronted with the inadequacy of their existing mental models. This approach aligns with pedagogical principles emphasizing active engagement with conflicting ideas as a mechanism for conceptual change.

% ============================================================================
% Subsection 2.2.4: Middle School Misconceptions About Matter Cycles and Conservation
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Middle School Misconceptions About Matter Cycles and Conservation}

At the middle school level, Arslan (2023) employed a word association test to investigate 457 eighth-grade students' cognitive perceptions and misconceptions regarding matter cycles and environmental phenomena. The study revealed widespread misconceptions about evaporation, condensation, and the conservation of matter during state changes, particularly among students in lower-performing schools with limited access to interactive learning materials. These findings underscore how resource constraints in Philippine schools compound the challenge of teaching abstract science concepts and exacerbate existing achievement gaps. The representation and presentation of scientific concepts significantly influence student understanding, particularly when integrating multiple representational modes---text, diagrams, animations, and interactive manipulatives---to support more robust conceptual development.

\section{Game-Based Learning}

% ============================================================================
% Subsection 2.3.1: Developmental Strategies of Edugames
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Developmental Strategies of Edugames}

Constructivist Learning Theory asserts that knowledge is actively constructed through interaction and experience rather than passively received. Game-based environments embody constructivist principles by placing learners in active problem-solving roles. In platform-based game environments, players experiment, test strategies, and receive immediate feedback from system responses. This aligns with the constructivist view that learning occurs through engagement and reflection upon action \cite{Kolb1984, Bruner1966}.

Outcome-based methodologies provide a structured framework for developing game-based learning environments aligned with explicit educational competencies. Sison and colleagues (2018) presented a formal Outcomes-Based Edugame Design methodology that operationalizes this alignment through a systematic process \cite{Sison2018}. This methodology begins by explicitly determining learning outcomes (LOs), selecting an appropriate game genre, and writing a game premise that contextualizes the learning objectives. Subsequently, the design phase proceeds incrementally through iterative ``Build Game Learning Environment (GLE)'' cycles, where each cycle focuses on designing a learning outcome mechanic, integrating related game elements, connecting with previously designed mechanics, and conducting playtesting and evaluation. This process repeats for each learning outcome before final integration of all elements and comprehensive evaluation \cite{Sison2018}. This approach ensures that gameplay mechanics directly support the achievement of specified educational outcomes, creating coherence between instructional goals and interactive gameplay.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{OutcomesBasedEdugameDesign.png}
\caption{Outcomes-Based Edugame Design Methodology (Sison et al., 2018)}
\label{fig:outcomes_edugame_design}
\end{figure}

In game development research, iterative design is a widely accepted approach for refining gameplay mechanics, usability, and user experience \cite{Gee2003}. Iterative development involves repeated cycles of testing, evaluation, modification, and regression validation. Campaign testing refers to structured playthrough sessions aimed at identifying usability issues, progression bottlenecks, and gameplay friction points. Engagement evaluation models assess multiple dimensions including focused attention, satisfaction, perceived learning, and intrinsic motivation. User-centered design principles emphasize incorporating player feedback into successive iterations to improve overall game quality \cite{Prensky2001}. The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design methodology institutionalizes this iterative approach by embedding playtesting and evaluation at every stage of development \cite{Sison2018}, ensuring that pedagogical alignment is maintained throughout the design process rather than evaluated only at the end. Educational game development should adopt a quasi-experimental assessment approach with pre- and post-gameplay measures to evaluate learning effectiveness, employing both quantitative metrics (test scores, learning gains) and qualitative methods (engagement surveys, observational feedback) to comprehensively evaluate user experience and educational impact.

\subsection{Effectiveness of games in education}

Multimedia Learning Theory suggests that learning is enhanced when verbal and visual information are presented in coordinated and complementary ways \cite{Mayer2001, Mayer2009}. Effective multimedia design integrates text, visuals, and animation to support meaningful cognitive processing. Research specifically demonstrates that animations with textual labels significantly enhance learning outcomes in scientific domains where direct observation is limited \cite{MayerMoreno2002}. Educational games that integrate explicit textual labeling (e.g., ``Solid $\rightarrow$ Liquid: Melting'') alongside dynamic visualization can support the formation of coherent mental representations of abstract processes.

Game-Based Learning Theory emphasizes the role of challenge, feedback, and goal-oriented interaction in promoting learner engagement \cite{Prensky2001}. Educational games combine instructional objectives with gameplay mechanics to foster sustained attention and intrinsic motivation. Engagement is a critical factor in educational technology adoption. Games that balance challenge and usability are more likely to maintain player interest and promote exploratory interaction \cite{Gee2003}. Assessment of engagement using structured instruments can determine whether a game builds demonstrates positive user experience.

Experiential Learning Theory posits that learning occurs through cycles of experience, reflection, conceptualization, and experimentation \cite{Kolb1984}. Simulated environments allow learners to experience phenomena that may not be directly observable in traditional classrooms. By simulating molecular interactions during freezing, melting, and evaporation, educational games can provide experiential exposure to abstract scientific processes aligned with curriculum standards. Outcome-Based Teaching and Learning emphasizes alignment between instructional activities and clearly defined competencies. Game level design can encourage players to apply conceptual knowledge to solve in-game challenges, aligning with higher-order cognitive objectives in frameworks such as Bloom's Taxonomy \cite{Anderson2001}.

\subsection{Game-Based Learning in the Philippines}

International research has demonstrated the effectiveness of educational games in enhancing engagement and conceptual understanding in science subjects. Studies evaluating interactive simulations and digital learning games report improvements in learner motivation and positive user experience when multimedia elements and active manipulation are integrated. Additionally, research on usability evaluation models such as MEEGA+KIDS highlights the importance of assessing engagement, focused attention, satisfaction, and perceived learning in educational game contexts \cite{vonWangenheim2020}. Studies on iterative game development emphasize that structured playtesting contributes significantly to improved playability, stability, and user satisfaction \cite{Sison2018}.

Local studies in educational technology within the Philippine context indicate increasing integration of digital learning tools aligned with curriculum standards. Research evaluating instructional games commonly employs usability surveys and engagement instruments to assess learner reception. However, limited research combines structured iterative development practices with formal engagement evaluation within educational game development projects in the Philippine context. This gap supports the relevance of developing and evaluating curriculum-aligned educational games that address the learning needs of Filipino students while demonstrating rigorous development and evaluation methodologies.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This study is grounded in a hierarchical integration of theoretical frameworks that collectively explain both the pedagogical rationale for game-based learning and the methodological approach employed in development and evaluation.

\subsubsection{Tier 1: Primary Theoretical Anchors}

Conceptual Change Theory serves as the primary foundation, particularly given the documented misconceptions about phase changes identified in the research literature \cite{Posner1982}. This theory posits that learners construct initial mental models of phenomena through everyday experiences; these models often conflict with scientifically accurate explanations. Conceptual change occurs not through passive information reception, but through active confrontation with the inadequacy of existing models \cite{Posner1982}. In the context of this study, students hold misconceptions about particle behavior during phase changes; the game is designed to create problem-solving scenarios that expose the limitations of these mental models and provide experiential evidence supporting more accurate particle-level explanations (Samarapungavan et al., 2023).

Constructivism complements Conceptual Change Theory by emphasizing that knowledge is actively constructed through interaction, experience, and reflection rather than passively received \cite{Bruner1966, Kolb1984}. In a constructivist game environment, learners do not receive explicit lectures on phase changes; instead, they manipulate particle spacing and bond strength through the interactive particle-lattice minigame, discovering the relationships between these manipulations and observable state transitions. This active engagement supports the conceptual change process by anchoring abstract ideas (particle motion) to concrete, manipulable experiences within the game \cite{Kolb1984}.

Multimedia Learning Theory addresses how to represent abstract concepts through coordinated presentation of multiple information channels \cite{Mayer2001, Mayer2009}. The game integrates text labels (e.g., ``Solid to Liquid: Melting''), animated particle representations, and interactive mechanics to convey phase change concepts. Research demonstrates that coordinated text-visual-animation presentations enhance learning outcomes compared to single-modality instruction, particularly for abstract domains where direct observation is limited \cite{MayerMoreno2002}. By simultaneously showing particle lattice structures, temperature effects, and textual terminology, the game leverages multimedia principles to support learner comprehension of microscopic phenomena.

\subsubsection{Tier 2: Supporting Theories}

Game-Based Learning Theory justifies the use of challenge-based interactive mechanics to foster sustained engagement \cite{Prensky2001, Gee2003}. This theory emphasizes that games combine immediate feedback, clear goals, balanced challenge-to-skill ratios, and intrinsic motivation mechanisms. In Wiz's Journey, these elements manifest as: (1) instant feedback when particles respond to player manipulations, (2) explicit win/fail conditions for phase change challenges, (3) progressive difficulty scaling, and (4) reward systems that reinforce both achievement and conceptual exploration \cite{Prensky2001}.

Experiential Learning Theory supports the use of simulated environments for learning abstract scientific processes \cite{Kolb1984}. This theory describes learning as occurring through cyclical experience-reflection-conceptualization-experimentation. The game environment allows learners to experience phase change phenomena that cannot be directly observed (molecular-level particle motion), reflect on the outcomes of their manipulations, form conceptual understanding of particle behavior, and experimentally test these understandings in subsequent challenges. This cycle repeats across levels, deepening conceptual sophistication \cite{Kolb1984}.

\subsubsection{Tier 3: Operational Frameworks}

Outcome-Based Teaching and Learning (OBTL) principles ensure alignment between pedagogical theory and curricular competencies \cite{Sison2018}. The game's learning outcome mechanics (LOMs) are explicitly designed to target specific competencies from the MATATAG Grade 6 Science curriculum, ensuring that gameplay directly supports achievement of standards-based objectives rather than generic skill development.

Bloom's Taxonomy guides cognitive level alignment \cite{Anderson2001}. Lower-order objectives (remember, understand) are addressed through labeled particle representations and discovery mechanics; higher-order objectives (apply, analyze) are addressed through complex multi-phase challenges requiring strategic particle manipulation to solve environmental puzzles. This hierarchical alignment ensures the game scaffolds learning from foundational understanding to sophisticated application.

Iterative Design Principles and User-Centered Development Frameworks justify the two-phase approach (Section 2.5). Rather than conducting summative evaluation immediately after development, the methodology incorporates repeated cycles of playtesting, feedback integration, and refinement \cite{Gee2003, Prensky2001}. This approach recognizes that pedagogical theory informs initial design, but actual player experiences reveal implementation gaps; iterative cycles resolve these gaps before formal evaluation, ensuring the final game genuinely embodies the theoretical principles that justify its use \cite{Sison2018}. The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design methodology \cite{Sison2018} formalizes this iterative process by requiring playtesting and evaluation after each learning outcome mechanic is designed, creating multiple feedback loops that systematically align pedagogical theory with gameplay implementation.

In synthesis, these frameworks operate hierarchically: Primary theories explain the deep pedagogical mechanisms (why games can facilitate conceptual change through constructivist interaction and multimedia learning); supporting theories specify motivational and experiential mechanisms (how games sustain engagement through challenge and experiential cycles); and operational frameworks translate these mechanisms into specific game features (which competencies to target and how to align gameplay to those targets). Iterative development ensures theoretical alignment, while structured evaluation validates achievement of alignment.

\subsection{Input Component}

The Input component comprises three integrated elements:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Foundations}: Conceptual Change, Constructivism, Multimedia Learning, Game-Based Learning, and Experiential Learning theories (described in Section 2.4) provide the pedagogical justification for game-based intervention. These theories collectively explain why interactive gameplay can support understanding of abstract phase change phenomena when traditional instruction alone proves insufficient. The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design methodology \cite{Sison2018} operationalizes these theoretical foundations by requiring explicit alignment of each learning outcome mechanic with underlying pedagogical principles.
    
    \item \textbf{Curricular Alignment}: The MATATAG Grade 6 Science competency on changes of state defines the content scope. Bloom's Taxonomy and Outcome-Based Teaching principles establish cognitive level expectations and learning outcome targets \cite{Anderson2001}. This ensures the game reinforces competencies students are expected to master rather than tangential content. The Sison et al. (2018) methodology \cite{Sison2018} begins with explicit determination of learning outcomes, ensuring curricular alignment from the inception of design.
    
    \item \textbf{User-Centered Design Principles}: Iterative design, playtesting feedback loops, and engagement evaluation models (such as MEEGA+KIDS) guide the development process \cite{vonWangenheim2020}. These principles ensure the game is not only pedagogically sound in theory but also usable, engaging, and motivating in practice. The Sison et al. (2018) methodology \cite{Sison2018} systematically embeds user testing through playtesting at every development stage, preventing theory-practice misalignment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Process Component}

The Process component is deliberately divided into two sequential phases, each with distinct purposes:

\subsubsection{Iterative Development Phase (Internal Refinement)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Campaign testing}: Systematic playthrough of game levels to identify progression issues, accessibility barriers, and gameplay friction
    \item \textbf{Regression testing}: Verification that modifications made in response to feedback do not reintroduce previously resolved issues
    \item \textbf{Playtesting feedback integration}: Incorporation of player observations, difficulty reports, and suggestions into game mechanics and usability improvements
    \item \textbf{Gameplay refinement}: Iterative modification of particle mechanics, level difficulty curves, narrative elements, and UI design based on accumulated feedback
\end{itemize}

This phase prioritizes alignment between pedagogical theory (Inputs) and actual player experience. For example, if Constructivist principles suggest players should discover phase relationships through experimentation, but playtesting reveals players are confused by ambiguous feedback mechanisms, refinements clarify feedback without resorting to explicit instruction, preserving the constructivist approach while improving comprehension. The Outcomes-Based Edugame Design methodology \cite{Sison2018} institutionalizes this alignment process through incremental game learning environment construction: each learning outcome mechanic is designed, integrated with related elements, connected to previously designed mechanics, and then immediately playtested and evaluated (Figure 2.1). This staged approach ensures that pedagogical alignment is validated at every step of development rather than only at the conclusion.

\subsubsection{Final Evaluation Phase (Summative Assessment)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured play session using finalized build}: Controlled gameplay environment with Grade 6 participants
    \item \textbf{MEEGA+KIDS usability and engagement assessment}: Quantitative measurement of focused attention, satisfaction, perceived learning, and intrinsic motivation dimensions \cite{vonWangenheim2020}
    \item \textbf{Qualitative feedback collection}: Post-play interviews or surveys capturing student perceptions of learning value, difficulty appropriateness, and enjoyment
\end{itemize}

This phase validates whether the refined game achieves its intended educational and motivational objectives.

\subsection{Output Component}

The Output component captures both developmental and evaluative results:

\subsubsection{Developmental Outputs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Improved gameplay stability and reduced technical issues
    \item Refined user experience with reduced navigation friction and clarity-enhanced feedback mechanisms
    \item Optimized difficulty progression supporting both accessibility and appropriate challenge
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluative Outputs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Measured engagement levels across MEEGA+KIDS dimensions indicating learner motivation and perceived learning
    \item Evaluated usability confirming Grade 6 students can successfully operate game mechanics without excessive frustration
    \item Evidence of conceptual understanding gains through pre-post assessment comparisons (evaluated in later research phases beyond this thesis scope)
\end{itemize}

The framework illustrates a systematic progression: theoretical foundations inform initial design, iterative development resolves implementation gaps, and final evaluation confirms both the game's usability and the validity of the theoretical approach. Importantly, the separation of iterative development from summative evaluation ensures that classroom-ready game builds undergo rigorous user testing before formal assessment, maximizing likelihood that measured outcomes reflect genuine intervention effectiveness rather than usability limitations masking pedagogical potential.
